One picture of Malaysian waters

Heimdall puts live ship positions, satellite radar and alerts for vessels that go dark on one map of the whole Malaysian EEZ, from the Strait of Malacca to east of Sabah. A named analyst approves every alert before it goes anywhere.

What it watches

Built on public and open data, so it works from day one without new sensors.

Live ships

Every vessel broadcasting AIS in Malaysian waters, updated by the minute, with its track.

Satellite radar

Sentinel-1 radar sees ships through cloud and at night, whether or not they broadcast.

Dark-vessel alerts

Groups of radar contacts with no broadcast, and ships that switch their AIS off.

Analyst approval

Every alert is approved or dismissed by a named person, and the decision is kept.

See what isn't broadcasting

Most vessels announce themselves over AIS. Heimdall compares that with what the satellites actually see. When radar finds a group of vessels that nothing accounts for, it raises an alert with the evidence: where, when, how many, and how many look like fishing boats.

Likely fishing boats can be filtered out, so an analyst's queue holds the contacts that matter to them.

Vessels and patterns, not people

Heimdall watches ships and what they do at sea. It doesn't track individuals and it doesn't target anything. It's built for the agencies and operators responsible for Malaysian waters: maritime enforcement, the navy, fisheries and port authorities.

Built on open data

Live AIS, Copernicus Sentinel-1 radar through Global Fishing Watch, and Marine Regions boundaries. Briefings show Heimdall on the live picture of Malaysian waters.
