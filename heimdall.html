See the boats that don't want to be seen

Heimdall is a maritime surveillance service for Malaysian waters. It watches the whole EEZ, from the Strait of Malacca to east of Sabah, for the patterns behind illegal fishing and drug smuggling: vessels running dark, fleets working where they shouldn't, and ships that switch off their transponders. Every case goes to a named analyst before anyone acts on it.

The sea is big. Patrols are few.

Malaysia's exclusive economic zone is about as large as its land, with some of the world's busiest shipping lanes running through them. Foreign fleets fish inside the EEZ without permission, and smugglers use the same water at night to move drugs between boats and the shore.

Most honest vessels announce themselves over AIS. The ones doing something wrong often don't. Patrol boats and aircraft can't be everywhere, so the question is where to send them.

Heimdall answers where to look

It compares what ships broadcast with what radar satellites actually see, day or night and through cloud, and turns the differences into a short list of cases. Each one comes with its evidence, its position relative to the nearest town, and a reason it was raised.

Analysts decide which cases are worth a patrol. Heimdall never acts on its own.

Two missions

The same picture of the sea, read two ways.

Illegal fishing

Foreign trawlers and purse seiners that work Malaysian waters without permission rarely broadcast where they are. Heimdall finds dense groups of radar contacts with no broadcast, and tells analysts how many of them look like fishing boats, so a fleet stands out from scattered traffic.

  • Groups of dark contacts inside the EEZ
  • Likely fishing boats, classified from radar
  • Fishing vessels that went quiet inside Malaysian waters

Drug smuggling

Smuggling at sea relies on not being noticed: transponders off, meetings after dark, boats that don't match any broadcast. Heimdall flags the vessels that switch AIS off on purpose and the contacts radar sees that nothing accounts for, along the coasts and straits where it matters.

  • Ships that switched off AIS, and for how long
  • Radar contacts with no broadcast, near the coast
  • Each case placed against the nearest town and its track

What a case looks like

"8 radar contacts with no AIS broadcast within 11 km, 102 km south-east of Mersing, 16 October." The contacts are marked on the map, alongside every ship that was broadcasting nearby, so an analyst can see at a glance whether it's a fleet, a convoy or noise.

Likely fishing boats can be hidden when the job is smuggling, and shown when it's fisheries enforcement.

From a satellite pass to a patrol

Heimdall finds. A person decides.

  1. 1

    Seen

    Radar satellites pass over Malaysian waters and detect vessels, day or night, through cloud.

  2. 2

    Matched

    Every contact is checked against the ships broadcasting AIS at the time. The ones left over are dark.

  3. 3

    Raised

    Dense groups of dark contacts, and ships that switch AIS off, become cases with their evidence.

  4. 4

    Decided

    A named analyst approves or dismisses each case, with a note. Every decision is kept, with who and when.
